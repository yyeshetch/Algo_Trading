    The strongest evidence is not actually for a particular candlestick pattern. It is for momentum and trend persistence.

    AQR's century-long research finds time-series momentum/trend following was profitable across many asset classes and economic environments going back to 1880.

    Research on individual stocks also finds substantial evidence for time-series momentum over very long samples.

    And momentum research isn't merely academic trivia: work by Baltas and Kosowski found that time-series momentum benchmarks explain a substantial portion of CTA/trend-following fund returns.

    2. Strategy #1 — Relative-strength momentum

    This is the foundation I'd use.

    The basic idea:

    Don't ask "Is this stock cheap?"

    Ask:

    "Is this stock materially stronger than almost everything else I could buy?"

    O'Neil built his methodology around price strength, earnings growth and institutional demand. His system popularized tools such as Relative Strength Rating and EPS Rating.

    Screen

    For Indian equities, I'd start with:

    • Price > ₹100
    • Average daily traded value > ₹5–10 crore
    • 20-day return > 0
    • 3-month return in top 20–30%
    • 6-month return in top 20–30%
    • 12-month return in top 20–30%
    • Stock > 50-day moving average
    • Stock > 200-day moving average
    • 50 DMA > 200 DMA
    • Relative strength versus Nifty improving
    • Relative strength versus sector improving
    • Volume increasing on advances

    Then rank the survivors.

    Don't buy everything that passes.

    The purpose is to create a leader universe.

    3. Strategy #2 — Breakout from a proper base

    This is one of the cleanest swing setups.

    Think:

    Trend → consolidation → volatility contracts → supply disappears → breakout → expansion

    Examples:

    • Cup & handle
    • Flat base
    • Volatility contraction pattern
    • Tight flag
    • Rectangle
    • Ascending base
    • Reaccumulation
    • IPO base

    O'Neil's methodology emphasizes constructive bases and breakouts, while Minervini's VCP specifically focuses on contracting volatility before expansion. Minervini's published material describes the VCP as a core part of his approach and emphasizes precise pivot entries and risk control.

    The ideal setup

    You want:

    1. Strong prior uptrend.
    2. Stock pulls sideways rather than collapses.
    3. Corrections become smaller.
    4. Volume dries up during contraction.
    5. Stock remains above major moving averages.
    6. Relative strength stays strong.
    7. Market/sector is healthy.
    8. Price approaches resistance.
    9. Breakout occurs with meaningful volume.
    10. Price closes strongly rather than immediately reversing.

    Entry

    Don't simply buy because price touches resistance.

    A more robust trigger:

    Buy when price clears the pivot/resistance with confirmation.

    For example:

    Pivot = ₹1,000
    Entry = ₹1,005–₹1,015 depending on liquidity/volatility
    Stop = structural invalidation below the setup

    The exact percentages should be tested rather than treated as magic numbers.

    4. Strategy #3 — Earnings + momentum

    This is one of the most powerful combinations.

    You want:

    Fundamental acceleration + price acceleration.

    Look for:

    • EPS growth accelerating
    • Revenue growth accelerating
    • Margin expansion
    • Positive earnings surprise
    • Strong guidance
    • Increasing institutional interest
    • Price already outperforming
    • Breakout after earnings
    • Sector itself strengthening

    The important distinction:

    Don't buy a great company simply because earnings are good.

    Buy when:

    good/improving fundamentals + market recognition + technical confirmation

    come together.

    IBD's methodology explicitly combines fundamental and technical analysis rather than treating an earnings trend alone as a buy signal.

    5. Strategy #4 — Post-earnings momentum

    This deserves its own system.

    A stock reports:

    Revenue +30%
    EPS +45%
    Guidance raised
    Price gaps +8%
    Volume 4× normal

    Most people chase the gap.

    A better approach is often:

    Let the market digest it.

    Then watch for:

    • Gap holds.
    • Price doesn't immediately fill the gap.
    • Volume contracts.
    • Price forms a short consolidation.
    • Relative strength remains extremely high.
    • Stock breaks the post-earnings range.

    That gives you:

    Information shock → institutional repricing → consolidation → continuation

    This is particularly useful for swing trading because you don't necessarily need to predict the earnings announcement.

    6. Strategy #5 — Wyckoff accumulation / reaccumulation

    This is the strategy I'd use when you want to understand what large participants may be doing, rather than simply looking at indicators.

    Wyckoff's framework revolves around:

    • Supply
    • Demand
    • Accumulation
    • Distribution
    • Springs
    • Tests
    • Signs of strength
    • Last points of support
    • Breakouts / "jumps"

    Modern Wyckoff practitioners still use price + volume + structure for swing trading. StockCharts, for example, describes accumulation structures where declining volume on reactions and increasing volume on rallies indicate diminishing supply and increasing demand.

    The setup

    Imagine:

    Decline → selling climax → automatic rally → test → sideways accumulation → higher lows → breakout

    You're trying to identify the transition:

    Supply > demand

    into

    Demand > supply

    This is particularly useful when combined with relative strength.

    7. Strategy #6 — Stage analysis

    Stan Weinstein's framework is beautifully simple.

    Think in four stages:

    Stage 1 — Base

    Stock moves sideways.

    Stage 2 — Advancing

    Price breaks upward and trend accelerates.

    This is where swing traders want to concentrate.

    Stage 3 — Distribution

    Trend becomes mature and unstable.

    Stage 4 — Decline

    Downtrend.

    The critical rule:

    Don't try to turn a Stage 4 stock into a Stage 2 stock because it looks cheap.

    For swing trading, I'd heavily favor:

    Stage 2 + strong relative strength + rising 30/50-week trend

    rather than trying to catch falling knives.

    8. Strategy #7 — Trend following / Turtle breakout

    This one has perhaps the strongest institutional pedigree.

    The famous Turtle experiment used systematic trend-following rules associated with Richard Dennis and William Eckhardt.

    The conceptual model is:

    Buy strength. Sell weakness. Let winners run. Cut losers.

    For example:

    Long

    Buy when:

    Close > highest close of previous N sessions

    Exit when:

    Close < lowest close of previous M sessions

    Position size is adjusted according to volatility.

    The exact original Turtle parameters aren't sacred. The important idea is:

    breakout + volatility-adjusted sizing + systematic exit.

    AQR's century study provides particularly strong independent evidence for the broader trend-following family.

    9. Strategy #8 — Volatility contraction → volatility expansion

    This is one of my favorite swing concepts.

    Markets alternate between:

    compression → expansion

    You can measure compression through:

    • ATR
    • Bollinger Band width
    • Historical volatility
    • True range
    • Candle ranges
    • Volume contraction

    You don't necessarily need an indicator.

    A very powerful visual sequence is:

    Large range → smaller range → smaller range → tiny range → breakout

    The key is that compression alone isn't bullish.

    You want:

    compression + strong trend + relative strength + support + demand

    Then the expansion becomes interesting.

    10. Strategy #9 — Pullback in a strong trend

    Instead of buying breakouts, wait for the leader to temporarily retreat.

    Ideal structure:

    Strong trend → controlled pullback → support → lower volume → reversal → continuation

    Possible support:

    • 10 EMA
    • 20 EMA
    • 21 EMA
    • 50 DMA
    • Previous breakout level
    • Prior resistance turned support
    • Anchored VWAP
    • Fibonacci retracement

    But don't turn these into magical numbers.

    The important thing is:

    A strong stock should pull back constructively.

    Weak stocks tend to collapse through support.

    11. Strategy #10 — Relative-strength sector rotation

    This is where the process becomes significantly better.

    Don't scan 5,000 stocks equally.

    First ask:

    Which sectors are winning?

    Then:

    Which stocks are winning inside those sectors?

    Example:

    Market → Sector → Industry → Stock

    Suppose:

    Nifty ↑
    → IT ↑↑
    → Midcap IT ↑↑↑
    → Stock A ↑↑↑↑

    Stock A deserves substantially more attention than a random stock showing the same chart pattern in a weak industry.

    Relative-strength analysis is also used by institutional technical researchers; Joe Rabil, whose research has been provided to large institutional money managers, uses multiple timeframes and momentum/relative-strength characteristics for stock and sector selection.

    12. Strategy #11 — Dual momentum

    This is more systematic.

    You combine:

    Absolute momentum

    Is the asset itself trending upward?

    with:

    Relative momentum

    Is it outperforming alternatives?

    For stocks:

    Stock return > 0

    AND

    Stock return > Nifty

    AND

    Stock return > sector

    This eliminates a huge number of mediocre candidates.

    Academic work has found time-series momentum in individual stocks and evidence supporting combinations of time-series and cross-sectional momentum.

    13. Strategy #12 — Mean reversion

    This is the one I'd put below momentum, not above it.

    The concept:

    Extremely oversold stocks sometimes bounce toward their mean.

    Potential setup:

    • Strong longer-term trend
    • Short-term sharp selloff
    • RSI extremely low
    • Price extended below short-term mean
    • Support nearby
    • Selling volume exhaustion
    • Market/sector not collapsing
    • Reversal candle / reclaim of short-term level

    The important distinction:

    Good mean reversion

    Pullback inside an uptrend.

    Dangerous mean reversion

    "It fell 70%, therefore it must bounce."

    Never confuse cheapness with mean reversion.

    21. Position sizing is more important than your entry

    Suppose your account is ₹10 lakh.

    Risk:

    0.5% = ₹5,000

    You enter at:

    ₹1,000

    Stop:

    ₹950

    Risk/share:

    ₹50

    Position:

    ₹5,000 / ₹50 = 100 shares

    Position value:

    ₹1,00,000

    So you're risking:

    0.5% of the account

    —not ₹1 lakh.

    This is the difference between:

    "I think this stock will go up."

    and

    "I know exactly how much I'm willing to lose if I'm wrong."

    22. Don't use stops blindly

    This is important.

    A stop isn't necessarily guaranteed to execute at the exact price.

    The SEC explains that when a stop price is reached, a stop order becomes a market order, and execution can differ materially from the stop price in fast markets.

    The SEC/CFTC material also highlights how clusters of stop orders can contribute to rapid liquidity removal and extreme execution conditions.

    So your risk model should account for gap risk/slippage, especially in volatile small caps.

    23. The exit system

    This is where most retail traders destroy good strategies.

    Don't use:

    "I'll sell when I feel like it."

    Use rules.

    Exit 1 — Thesis failure

    Breaks the level that invalidates the setup.

    Exit.

    Exit 2 — Abnormal volume reversal

    Huge volume + failed breakout + close near lows.

    Potential exit.

    Exit 3 — Relative strength deterioration

    If the market is strong but your stock suddenly becomes weak:

    warning.

    Exit 4 — Trend violation

    Repeated closes below important support / moving average.

    Reduce or exit depending on system.

    Exit 5 — Trailing stop

    Once the stock makes a substantial move, trail based on:

    • swing lows
    • ATR
    • moving average
    • previous breakout levels

    Exit 6 — Climactic move

    Parabolic price acceleration + huge volume + extreme extension.

    Start taking profits rather than expecting infinity.

    Minervini's methodology places substantial emphasis on sell rules, stop management, position sizing and post-trade analysis.

    24. Your target should NOT be "2:1"

    This is another oversimplification.

    A trend-following strategy might have:

    35–45% winners

    and still make a lot of money because:

    winners = huge
    losers = small

    A mean-reversion strategy might have:

    65–75% winners

    but still lose money if the losers are catastrophic.

    The statistic you should optimize is:

    Expectancy

    Expectancy = (Win rate × Average win) − (Loss rate × Average loss)

    Example:

    45% winners
    Average winner = +4R
    55% losers
    Average loser = −1R

    Expected result:

    0.45 × 4 − 0.55 × 1 = +1.25R

    That's a potentially excellent system despite losing more trades than it wins.

    28. Then read the academic evidence

    This is where you separate real edges from trading mythology.

    AQR — Trend following

    Probably the single most useful research paper for understanding why trend following deserves serious attention. It studies trend following across markets back to 1880.

    AQR — Factor premia

    Covers momentum, value, carry and defensive factors across a century and multiple asset classes. The authors also caution that attempts to dynamically time factors can lose profitability after transaction costs and proper data lags.

    29. The most important distinction

    You asked for market leaders, market makers, institutional traders and hedge fund managers.

    There's a subtle problem here.

    A market maker's edge is generally:

    liquidity provision + order flow + inventory management + microstructure

    A CTA's edge is:

    systematic trend following

    A growth-stock trader's edge is:

    fundamental acceleration + price momentum + institutional demand

    A Wyckoff trader's framework is:

    supply/demand + accumulation/distribution

    A hedge fund quant might use:

    momentum + quality + volatility + liquidity + portfolio construction

    These aren't the same strategy.

    Trying to combine all of them into:

    "RSI + MACD + VWAP + Bollinger Bands + FII + Fibonacci"

    doesn't make you institutional.

    It usually makes the model overfit.

<section>
  <h2>2. Relative-Strength Momentum</h2>

  <p>
    The core question is not:
    <em>"Is this stock cheap?"</em>
  </p>

  <p>
    Instead:
    <strong>"Is this stock materially stronger than most of the stocks I
    could buy?"</strong>
  </p>

  <h3>Example Screening Criteria</h3>

  <ul>
    <li>Price above ₹100</li>
    <li>Average daily traded value above ₹5–10 crore</li>
    <li>20-day return positive</li>
    <li>3-month return in the top 20–30%</li>
    <li>6-month return in the top 20–30%</li>
    <li>12-month return in the top 20–30%</li>
    <li>Price above 50 DMA</li>
    <li>Price above 200 DMA</li>
    <li>50 DMA above 200 DMA</li>
    <li>Relative strength versus Nifty improving</li>
    <li>Relative strength versus sector improving</li>
    <li>Volume increasing on advances</li>
  </ul>

  <p>
    The purpose is to create a <strong>leader universe</strong>, not to
    blindly buy every stock that passes.
  </p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>3. Breakout From a Proper Base</h2>

  <p>
    A high-quality breakout often follows:
  </p>

  <pre>Strong Trend
     ↓
Consolidation
     ↓
Volatility Contracts
     ↓
Supply Dries Up
     ↓
Breakout
     ↓
Volume / Price Expansion</pre>

  <p>Examples include:</p>

  <ul>
    <li>Cup and handle</li>
    <li>Flat base</li>
    <li>Volatility contraction pattern</li>
    <li>Tight flag</li>
    <li>Rectangle</li>
    <li>Ascending base</li>
    <li>Reaccumulation</li>
    <li>IPO base</li>
  </ul>

  <h3>Ideal Setup</h3>

  <ol>
    <li>Strong prior uptrend.</li>
    <li>Stock consolidates rather than collapses.</li>
    <li>Corrections become smaller.</li>
    <li>Volume dries up during contraction.</li>
    <li>Price remains above major moving averages.</li>
    <li>Relative strength remains strong.</li>
    <li>Sector is healthy.</li>
    <li>Price approaches resistance.</li>
    <li>Breakout occurs with meaningful volume.</li>
    <li>Price closes strongly.</li>
  </ol>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>6. Wyckoff Accumulation / Reaccumulation</h2>

  <p>
    Wyckoff provides a framework for interpreting price and volume through
    supply and demand.
  </p>

  <ul>
    <li>Accumulation</li>
    <li>Distribution</li>
    <li>Springs</li>
    <li>Tests</li>
    <li>Signs of strength</li>
    <li>Last points of support</li>
    <li>Breakouts</li>
  </ul>

  <pre>Decline
   ↓
Selling Climax
   ↓
Automatic Rally
   ↓
Test
   ↓
Accumulation
   ↓
Higher Lows
   ↓
Breakout
   ↓
Markup</pre>

  <p>
    The objective is to identify a transition from:
    <strong>Supply &gt; Demand</strong>
    to
    <strong>Demand &gt; Supply</strong>.
  </p>
</section>

<section id="risk">
  <h2>23. Position Sizing</h2>

  <p>
    Position size should be determined by the amount you're willing to lose,
    not by how much capital you have available.
  </p>

  <div class="card">
    <h3>Example</h3>

    <p>Account = ₹10,00,000</p>
    <p>Risk per trade = 0.5% = ₹5,000</p>
    <p>Entry = ₹1,000</p>
    <p>Stop = ₹950</p>
    <p>Risk/share = ₹50</p>
    <p>Position size = ₹5,000 / ₹50 = <strong>100 shares</strong></p>
    <p>Position value = ₹1,00,000</p>
  </div>

  <p>
    You're therefore risking approximately <strong>0.5% of the account</strong>,
    rather than simply risking the entire ₹1 lakh position.
  </p>

  <div class="card danger">
    <strong>Account for slippage and gap risk.</strong>
    A stop price does not necessarily guarantee execution at that exact price,
    particularly in fast-moving or illiquid markets.
  </div>
</section>

<section id="exit">
  <h2>24. Exit System</h2>

  <h3>Exit 1 — Thesis Failure</h3>
  <p>
    Price breaks the level that invalidates the setup.
  </p>

  <h3>Exit 2 — Failed Breakout</h3>
  <p>
    Large volume reversal and close back into the base can indicate failure.
  </p>

  <h3>Exit 3 — Relative-Strength Deterioration</h3>
  <p>
    If the market remains strong while the stock becomes materially weaker,
    treat it as a warning.
  </p>

  <h3>Exit 4 — Trend Violation</h3>
  <p>
    Repeated closes below important structural support may justify reducing
    or exiting the position.
  </p>

  <h3>Exit 5 — Trailing Stop</h3>

  <ul>
    <li>Swing lows</li>
    <li>ATR</li>
    <li>Moving averages</li>
    <li>Previous breakout levels</li>
  </ul>

  <h3>Exit 6 — Climactic Move</h3>
  <p>
    Parabolic acceleration combined with extreme volume and extension can be
    a reason to begin taking profits or tightening the trailing mechanism.
  </p>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>25. Expectancy</h2>

  <p>
    Don't judge a strategy solely by its win rate.
  </p>

  <pre>Expectancy =
(Win Rate × Average Win)
−
(Loss Rate × Average Loss)</pre>

  <div class="card score">
    <p>Example:</p>
    <p>Win rate = 45%</p>
    <p>Average winner = +4R</p>
    <p>Loss rate = 55%</p>
    <p>Average loser = −1R</p>

    <h3>Expectancy = +1.25R</h3>
  </div>

  <p>
    A strategy can therefore be profitable even if it loses more trades than
    it wins, provided its winners are sufficiently larger than its losers.
  </p>
</section>

<section id="workflow">
  <h2>30. Weekly Trading Workflow</h2>

  <h3>Saturday / Sunday</h3>

  <ol>
    <li>Determine market regime.</li>
    <li>Rank sectors.</li>
    <li>Run stock scans.</li>
    <li>Check fundamental acceleration.</li>
    <li>Check institutional participation.</li>
    <li>Manually review charts.</li>
    <li>Reduce the universe to 5–15 candidates.</li>
    <li>Prepare entry/stop/position-size plans.</li>
    <li>Set alerts.</li>
  </ol>

  <h3>During the Week</h3>

  <ol>
    <li>Do not chase extended moves.</li>
    <li>Wait for the predefined trigger.</li>
    <li>Calculate position size before entering.</li>
    <li>Place risk controls.</li>
    <li>Track relative strength.</li>
    <li>Manage winners according to predefined rules.</li>
    <li>Record every trade.</li>
  </ol>

  <h3>End of Week</h3>

  <ul>
    <li>Review every trade.</li>
    <li>Review missed setups.</li>
    <li>Measure expectancy.</li>
    <li>Measure slippage.</li>
    <li>Identify rule violations.</li>
    <li>Update the research database.</li>
  </ul>
</section>

<section>
  <h2>32. The Core Principle</h2>

  <div class="card score">
    <p>
      Don't search for stocks that merely <em>might</em> go up.
    </p>

    <p>
      Search for stocks that are:
    </p>

    <p>
      <strong>
        Already demonstrating unusual strength, in strong industries,
        with improving fundamentals, under constructive supply/demand
        conditions, approaching a technically favorable point where
        downside can be defined tightly.
      </strong>
    </p>
  </div>

  <p>
    The scanner finds candidates.
    The chart finds the setup.
    The risk model determines whether you can survive.
    The journal determines whether you actually have an edge.
  </p>
</section>
